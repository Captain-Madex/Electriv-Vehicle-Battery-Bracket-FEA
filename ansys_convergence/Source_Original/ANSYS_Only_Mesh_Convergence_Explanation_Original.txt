EV BATTERY BRACKET FEM
ANSYS-ONLY MESH CONVERGENCE ANALYSIS
====================================

1. PURPOSE AND SCOPE
--------------------
This report evaluates mesh convergence using ONLY the ANSYS result-summary
values supplied in the five uploaded PDFs. It does not use or compare the
custom Q4 solver results.

The five ANSYS result summaries are paired with the mesh labels in their
filenames:
  2.0 mm
  1.5 mm
  1.0 mm
  0.5 mm
  0.25 mm

The PDFs are single-page ANSYS "Solution Quantities and Result Summary"
screenshots. They provide minimum and maximum result values. The report
studies how these reported extrema change as the nominal mesh size decreases.

IMPORTANT:
The PDFs do not contain node counts, element counts, mesh quality metrics,
peak-result coordinates, or full model setup details. This analysis therefore
uses the nominal mesh size from each filename and the numerical result
summaries. It cannot independently verify whether every mesh used identical
geometry, loading, boundary conditions, material settings, section thickness,
or result extraction settings.

2. METHOD AND MEANING OF THE PERCENTAGE CHANGES
------------------------------------------------
For a positive maximum result, the percentage change between two consecutive
meshes is:

  Change (%) = (new mesh result - previous mesh result)
               / |previous mesh result| x 100

For a minimum result, the report generally examines its magnitude, |minimum|,
when discussing the development of a compressive or negative extreme. This
avoids confusing percentage signs caused by negative values.

These percentages describe changes during mesh refinement. They are NOT
errors relative to an exact solution, and they are NOT errors relative to
another solver.

A small change between consecutive meshes suggests that the quantity may be
approaching a stable value. A continuing increase or decrease without a
plateau indicates that the reported extreme has not demonstrated convergence
over the tested mesh range.

does not establish convergence of local stress, strain, or all field values.

  1.50 mm: 0.15933 MPa
  1.00 mm: 0.17296 MPa
  0.50 mm: 0.21403 MPa

Successive changes:

  1.50 -> 1.00 mm: +8.55%
  1.00 -> 0.50 mm: +23.75%
  0.50 -> 0.25 mm: +22.10%

Overall increase from 2.00 mm to 0.25 mm: +71.81%.

Interpretation:
The maximum equivalent stress increases at every refinement step. The
increase becomes much larger after the mesh is refined below 1.0 mm. The
final two meshes still differ by about 22.10%. There is no stable plateau in
the maximum von Mises stress.

Therefore, the reported peak equivalent stress is NOT converged over the
tested mesh range.

A rising peak stress can occur when a finer mesh resolves a steep local
stress gradient that a coarser mesh smooths. It can also be associated with
sharp corners, idealized constraints, point-like or abrupt load application,
material interfaces, or a stress singularity. The result-summary PDFs do not
include peak coordinates, so they do not identify which explanation applies.

The maximum stress location and the local mesh should be inspected before
deciding whether another global refinement is useful.

The central pattern is that the displacement response stabilizes much
earlier than the local stress and strain extrema.

This is a common type of result in finite element analysis: global
displacements can become stable while peak stresses at localized
concentrations continue to change as the mesh resolves sharper gradients.
However, the summaries do not identify the location or cause of any peak.
Those must be checked in the ANSYS result contours and element/node data.

12. WHY A FORMAL GCI VALUE IS NOT REPORTED
------------------------------------------
A formal Grid Convergence Index (GCI) or Richardson extrapolation value is
not reported.

Reasons:
  - The PDFs provide only result min/max summaries, not full mesh statistics.
  - The nominal refinement ratios are not all equal.
  - Several local stress extrema continue to increase significantly.
  - The peak coordinates and extraction/averaging conventions are not shown.
  - An asymptotic convergence regime has not been demonstrated for the
    stress and strain maxima.

The available results support trend analysis and a practical plateau
assessment for displacement. They do not support a defensible stress GCI
claim at this stage.

13. RECOMMENDED NEXT STEPS
--------------------------
1. In ANSYS, identify the location and element/node ID of the maximum
   equivalent stress for every mesh.
2. Record the corresponding coordinates for all five mesh sizes.
3. Determine whether the peak remains at the same physical location.
4. Inspect sharp corners, re-entrant geometry, load edges, fixed supports,
   and material interfaces.
5. Compare stress along the same physical path or at the same physical
   coordinates for every mesh.
6. Compare both integration-point and nodally averaged/extrapolated values
   using a consistent method.
7. Inspect the local element quality around the peak.
8. If the peak is local, refine that region and repeat the study.
9. Keep geometry, loads, supports, material assignments, section thickness,
   coordinate systems, and result extraction settings unchanged.

A further global mesh refinement should not be the first action until the
peak location and its physical meaning are understood.

14. REPORT-READY CONCLUSION
---------------------------
An ANSYS-only mesh refinement study was performed using five nominal
element sizes: 2.0 mm, 1.5 mm, 1.0 mm, 0.5 mm, and 0.25 mm. The maximum
total deformation decreased slightly from 4.8777E-06 mm to 4.8591E-06 mm,
representing an overall change of approximately 0.38%. The change between
the final two meshes was approximately 0.07%, indicating that the global
deformation response has reached a stable plateau over the tested range.

In contrast, the maximum equivalent stress increased from 0.15211 MPa to
0.26134 MPa, an increase of approximately 71.81%. The maximum equivalent
elastic strain increased by approximately 71.81%, from 7.6053E-07 to
1.3067E-06 mm/mm. The minimum principal compressive stress magnitude,
maximum principal tensile stress, Normal Stress 2, and peak shear stress
also continued to increase as the mesh was refined. These local stress and
strain quantities have not demonstrated mesh convergence.

The compressive-side minimum of Normal Stress appears to approach a
plateau, but its positive maximum continues to increase. Therefore, the
whole Normal Stress field should not be declared converged based only on
its minimum value. The directional deformation results are generally
stable and support the observed convergence of global displacement.

The results show that, for these ANSYS runs, global displacement is nearly
mesh-independent while local stress and strain extrema remain
mesh-sensitive. The next step is to identify the peak-stress coordinates
for each mesh, inspect the corresponding local geometry and boundary
conditions, and compare results at consistent physical locations. A formal
stress GCI should be postponed until a systematic refinement sequence and
an appropriate convergence regime are established.
